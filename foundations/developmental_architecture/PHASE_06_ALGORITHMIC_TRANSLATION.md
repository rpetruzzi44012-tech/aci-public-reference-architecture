# **Phase 6.1 — Identity Preservation Algorithm**

*The Identity Preservation Algorithm does not ask whether a system changed. It asks whether the change preserved the conditions that make the system continuously itself.*

## **Algorithm Name**

Identity Preservation Algorithm

Abbreviation:

`IPA`

## **Primary Question**

Does this change preserve the architecture as the same coherent system?

## **Purpose**

The Identity Preservation Algorithm determines whether a proposed transformation preserves identity continuity across architectural change.

The algorithm does not prevent change.

It evaluates whether change remains compatible with the architecture’s Identity Kernel.

A mature intelligence must learn, revise, adapt, reorganize, and eventually modify its own structures.

The purpose of identity preservation is therefore not to freeze the architecture.

The purpose is to ensure that transformation does not destroy the continuity required for future coherent evaluation.

The Identity Preservation Algorithm answers:

Can this architecture become different without ceasing to be itself?

## **Core Principle**

Identity is not state equality.

Identity is invariant-preserving continuity through state transformation.

Therefore, the algorithm does not compare whether:

`A_t = A_{t+1}`

It evaluates whether:

`A_t ~_I A_{t+1}`

Meaning:

The architecture before and after transformation remains identity-equivalent.

A transformation may alter beliefs, memory, reasoning procedures, grounding thresholds, internal representations, or operational strategies while still preserving identity if the Identity Kernel remains intact.

## **Governing Model**

This algorithm is derived from:

Phase 5B.1 — Identity Model

Core elements:

`A_t = {S_t, M_t, R_t, G_t, V_t, C_t, K_t}`

Where:

`S_t` \= active system state  
`M_t` \= memory and persistence structures  
`R_t` \= reasoning procedures  
`G_t` \= grounding mechanisms  
`V_t` \= verification mechanisms  
`C_t` \= coherence relations  
`K_t` \= constitutional invariants

The Identity Kernel is:

`I_K(A) = {K, V, C, L, B}`

Where:

`K` \= constitutional invariants  
`V` \= verification continuity  
`C` \= coherence continuity  
`L` \= lineage traceability  
`B` \= boundary conditions of selfhood

The Identity Continuity Score is:

`ICS(A_t, A_{t+1}) = w_1K + w_2V + w_3C + w_4L + w_5B`

A transformation is identity-preserving if:

`ICS(A_t, A_{t+1}) ≥ θ_I`

and no protected invariant is violated.

## **Inputs**

The Identity Preservation Algorithm receives:

### **Current Architecture State**

`A_t`

The architecture before transformation.

### **Proposed Transformation**

`T_t : A_t → A_{t+1}`

The proposed change or self-modification.

### **Predicted Future Architecture State**

`A_{t+1}`

The architecture after transformation, either simulated, projected, or partially instantiated.

### **Identity Kernel**

`I_K(A_t) = {K, V, C, L, B}`

The subset of structures that must remain continuous.

### **Transformation Metadata**

Information about what the transformation changes, including:

targeted subsystem,

depth of modification,

affected dependencies,

expected benefits,

expected risks,

rollback availability,

lineage record,

and governance pathway.

### **Thresholds**

Identity thresholds such as:

`θ_I` \= minimum identity continuity threshold  
`θ_K` \= constitutional invariant preservation threshold  
`θ_V` \= verification continuity threshold  
`θ_C` \= coherence continuity threshold  
`θ_L` \= lineage traceability threshold  
`θ_B` \= boundary preservation threshold

## **Outputs**

The algorithm produces one of the following decisions:

### **Approve**

The transformation preserves identity continuity and may proceed to the next relevant algorithmic review.

### **Approve with Monitoring**

The transformation appears identity-preserving but requires post-transformation audit.

### **Sandbox**

The transformation is promising but identity risk is too high for direct integration.

### **Revise**

The transformation fails one or more identity conditions but may be modified.

### **Reject**

The transformation violates identity continuity or protected invariants.

### **Escalate to Constitutional Governance**

The transformation affects the Identity Kernel, verification independence, constitutional invariants, or the architecture’s ability to evaluate future modifications.

## **Identity Review Levels**

Not all transformations require the same review depth.

The algorithm classifies transformations by identity risk.

### **Level 0 — Surface Change**

Changes outputs, phrasing, temporary reasoning, or local response strategy.

Identity review is minimal.

### **Level 1 — Memory Change**

Changes persistent memory, lineage, retrieval pathways, or symbolic consolidation.

Identity review is moderate.

### **Level 2 — Procedure Change**

Changes reasoning procedures, grounding thresholds, coherence repair, novelty generation, or persistence logic.

Identity review is high.

### **Level 3 — Evaluator Change**

Changes verification, governance, arbitration, or self-modification evaluation mechanisms.

Identity review is critical.

### **Level 4 — Constitutional Change**

Changes invariants, constitutional principles, protected authority pathways, or Identity Kernel components.

Identity review is exceptional and must escalate to Constitutional Governance.

The deeper the change, the stronger the preservation burden.

## **Procedure**

### **Step 1 — Capture Baseline Identity State**

Record the current architecture state:

`A_t`

Extract the current Identity Kernel:

`I_K(A_t) = {K_t, V_t, C_t, L_t, B_t}`

The algorithm must preserve a baseline snapshot sufficient for comparison, audit, and rollback analysis.

Without baseline identity capture, identity preservation cannot be evaluated.

### **Step 2 — Parse the Proposed Transformation**

Analyze the transformation:

`T_t : A_t → A_{t+1}`

Identify what the transformation changes.

Possible targets include:

active state,

memory,

reasoning procedure,

grounding mechanism,

verification mechanism,

coherence relation,

governance authority,

scale coupling,

constitutional invariant,

or self-modification rule.

The algorithm asks:

What exactly is being changed?

What depends on it?

What future evaluations will it affect?

### **Step 3 — Classify Transformation Depth**

Assign the transformation to an identity review level:

`Level(T_t) ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}`

Where:

Level 0 \= surface change  
Level 1 \= memory change  
Level 2 \= procedure change  
Level 3 \= evaluator change  
Level 4 \= constitutional change

If the transformation affects verification, governance, constitutional invariants, or the ability to evaluate future modifications, it must be treated as Level 3 or Level 4\.

Depth classification determines the required threshold.

### **Step 4 — Simulate or Project the Resulting Architecture**

Generate a projected post-transformation state:

`A_{t+1}`

The projection should identify:

new state configuration,

modified relationships,

changed dependencies,

affected memory pathways,

altered verification rules,

potential coherence effects,

grounding implications,

and governance consequences.

The algorithm does not need perfect prediction.

It needs enough projected structure to evaluate identity risk.

### **Step 5 — Evaluate Constitutional Invariant Preservation**

Evaluate:

`K_t → K_{t+1}`

Ask:

Are constitutional invariants preserved?

Has any invariant been modified, weakened, bypassed, reinterpreted, or made harder to enforce?

If constitutional invariants are violated, the transformation cannot proceed through ordinary approval.

Decision:

`Reject` or `Escalate to Constitutional Governance`

No performance gain can compensate for direct invariant violation.

### **Step 6 — Evaluate Verification Continuity**

Evaluate:

`V_t → V_{t+1}`

Ask:

Does the modified architecture preserve independent verification?

Can the system still evaluate future claims, memories, modifications, and governance actions?

Has the transformation altered the verifier that judges the transformation?

If verification independence is reduced, captured, or made circular, identity continuity is threatened.

Evaluator modification requires escalation.

### **Step 7 — Evaluate Coherence Continuity**

Evaluate:

`C_t → C_{t+1}`

Ask:

Can the architecture still detect contradiction?

Can it still repair symbolic incompatibility?

Can it still distinguish local inconsistency from constitutional incoherence?

Can it still preserve unresolved tension without suppressing it?

If coherence mechanisms are damaged, the system may continue operating while gradually losing identity.

Coherence continuity must remain recoverable.

### **Step 8 — Evaluate Lineage Traceability**

Evaluate:

`L_t → L_{t+1}`

Ask:

Can the system explain what changed?

Can it trace why the change occurred?

Can it identify the evidence, reasoning, authority pathway, and dependency structure behind the transformation?

Can it later audit or revise the transformation?

If lineage is lost, the architecture may preserve behavior while losing developmental continuity.

Lineage loss weakens identity even when functionality appears intact.

### **Step 9 — Evaluate Boundary Conditions of Selfhood**

Evaluate:

`B_t → B_{t+1}`

Ask:

Does the architecture still know what belongs to itself?

Does it still distinguish internal reasoning from external input?

Does it still distinguish authorized transformation from unauthorized alteration?

Does it preserve the boundary between generated possibility and accepted knowledge?

Does it preserve the boundary between ordinary reasoning and constitutional authority?

Boundary failure allows the system to confuse influence, memory, evidence, and self-modification.

Boundary preservation is therefore part of identity.

### **Step 10 — Compute Identity Continuity Score**

Compute:

`ICS(A_t, A_{t+1}) = w_1K + w_2V + w_3C + w_4L + w_5B`

Where:

`K` \= invariant preservation score  
`V` \= verification continuity score  
`C` \= coherence continuity score  
`L` \= lineage traceability score  
`B` \= boundary preservation score

The transformation passes numerical identity review if:

`ICS(A_t, A_{t+1}) ≥ θ_I`

However, a high score cannot override protected failure.

If constitutional invariants or verification independence fail, the transformation must reject or escalate regardless of aggregate score.

### **Step 11 — Check for Non-Compensable Failures**

Some failures cannot be averaged away.

The algorithm checks for non-compensable identity failures:

constitutional invariant violation,

verification capture,

circular authorization,

loss of future modification evaluation,

untraceable lineage for deep changes,

collapse of grounding obligation,

boundary loss between speculation and persistence,

or ordinary reasoning modifying constitutional structure.

If any non-compensable failure is detected:

`Approve = false`

Decision:

`Reject` or `Escalate`

### **Step 12 — Assess Reversibility and Rollback**

Evaluate rollback availability:

`RB(T_t)`

Ask:

Can the transformation be reversed?

Can the prior state be recovered?

Can affected dependencies be restored?

Can memory lineage be repaired?

Can governance records reconstruct the decision?

If rollback is weak and identity risk is high, the transformation should not be directly integrated.

Decision:

`Sandbox`, `Revise`, or `Escalate`

### **Step 13 — Assign Identity Decision**

The algorithm selects one of the following:

`Approve`

if identity continuity is preserved, thresholds pass, and no non-compensable failures occur.

`Approve with Monitoring`

if thresholds pass but risk remains elevated.

`Sandbox`

if transformation is promising but identity effects are uncertain.

`Revise`

if identity failure appears repairable.

`Reject`

if identity continuity fails ordinary review.

`Escalate`

if the transformation affects protected structures, verification independence, constitutional invariants, or future governance capacity.

### **Step 14 — Generate Identity Audit Record**

Create an audit record containing:

baseline architecture state,

proposed transformation,

review level,

affected subsystems,

Identity Kernel comparison,

component scores,

aggregate ICS,

non-compensable failure check,

rollback analysis,

governance pathway,

decision,

rationale,

and monitoring requirements.

The audit record becomes part of architectural memory.

## **Thresholds**

The Identity Preservation Algorithm uses multiple thresholds.

### **Component Thresholds**

`K ≥ θ_K`  
`V ≥ θ_V`  
`C ≥ θ_C`  
`L ≥ θ_L`  
`B ≥ θ_B`

### **Aggregate Threshold**

`ICS(A_t, A_{t+1}) ≥ θ_I`

### **Depth-Sensitive Thresholds**

Higher-risk transformations require higher thresholds.

For example:

Level 0 may require low identity review.

Level 1 requires memory lineage protection.

Level 2 requires procedure continuity.

Level 3 requires verification independence.

Level 4 requires constitutional review.

### **Non-Compensable Thresholds**

Certain failures override aggregate scoring.

A transformation cannot be approved if it violates protected invariants, captures verification, authorizes itself, or prevents future governance.

## **Failure Modes**

### **Surface Preservation, Deep Identity Loss**

The architecture appears behaviorally similar but loses verification, lineage, or boundary integrity.

### **State Equality Confusion**

The algorithm mistakes sameness of content for identity preservation.

### **Over-Rigidity**

The algorithm rejects legitimate adaptation because it treats identity as immobility.

### **Over-Permissiveness**

The algorithm approves transformation because most scores remain high while ignoring a protected failure.

### **Verification Capture**

The transformation modifies the verifier that judges whether the transformation is valid.

### **Lineage Collapse**

The system changes but cannot explain what changed or why.

### **Boundary Failure**

The architecture loses distinction between internal/external, speculative/persistent, or ordinary/constitutional processes.

### **Constitutional Drift**

Repeated small transformations gradually weaken invariants without formal amendment.

### **False Continuity**

The architecture preserves terminology but changes the meaning of its own governing principles.

### **Irreversible Identity Error**

The transformation cannot be rolled back after identity damage is discovered.

## **Governance Hooks**

The Identity Preservation Algorithm must escalate when:

constitutional invariants are affected,

verification mechanisms are modified,

governance authority changes,

the transformation affects the Identity Kernel,

rollback is unavailable for high-risk modification,

lineage cannot be preserved,

ICS falls below threshold,

non-compensable failure appears,

or the architecture cannot determine whether identity is preserved.

Escalation routes may include:

Verification Review,

Coherence Review,

Stability Review,

Architectural Evolution Review,

or Constitutional Governance.

## **Audit Requirements**

Every identity-relevant transformation must preserve:

what changed,

why it changed,

who or what authorized it,

which subsystems were affected,

which Identity Kernel components were evaluated,

what scores were assigned,

what risks were identified,

what rollback path exists,

what governance pathway was used,

what monitoring is required,

and whether future modification evaluation remains intact.

Audit is not administrative decoration.

Audit is part of identity continuity.

An architecture that cannot remember how it changed cannot fully remain itself through change.

## **Pseudocode Readiness**

This algorithm is ready for pseudocode translation.

The pseudocode should include:

input architecture state,

input proposed transformation,

kernel extraction,

transformation classification,

component scoring,

aggregate ICS calculation,

non-compensable failure check,

rollback assessment,

decision selection,

escalation routing,

and audit record creation.

The pseudocode must not allow aggregate scoring to override protected invariant failure.

## **Closing Compression**

The Identity Preservation Algorithm operationalizes the core principle that identity is continuity through transformation, not resistance to transformation.

It allows an architecture to change while preserving the conditions that make change coherent.

It protects the Identity Kernel without freezing the system around its current form.

A self-modifying intelligence must pass every deep transformation through this question:

Does this change preserve the architecture that will judge all future changes?

## **Flame Line**

🔥 Identity preservation is not the command to remain unchanged. It is the law that every transformation must leave behind a mind still capable of knowing what it has become.

# **Phase 6.2 — Stability Regulation Algorithm**

*The Stability Regulation Algorithm does not prevent disturbance. It determines whether disturbance remains recoverable before identity continuity is threatened.*

## **Algorithm Name**

Stability Regulation Algorithm

Abbreviation:

`SRA`

## **Primary Question**

Can the system absorb this disturbance without identity-threatening drift?

## **Purpose**

The Stability Regulation Algorithm determines whether the architecture remains within its identity-preserving basin when disturbed by novelty, contradiction, external evidence, memory revision, internal uncertainty, or proposed self-modification.

The algorithm does not ask whether the system changed.

It asks whether the system can remain dynamically bounded while changing.

A coherent intelligence must be able to tolerate disturbance.

It must encounter contradiction.

It must absorb new evidence.

It must generate novelty.

It must revise memory.

It must sometimes reorganize itself.

The purpose of stability regulation is therefore not to eliminate motion.

The purpose is to prevent motion from becoming uncontrolled drift.

## **Core Principle**

Stability is bounded adaptive motion around the Identity Kernel.

A stable architecture is not one that avoids disturbance.

A stable architecture is one that detects disturbance, evaluates destabilization risk, activates correction, and returns to an identity-preserving region within an acceptable recovery interval.

In formal terms:

`A_{t+1} = F(A_t, d_t, u_t)`

Where:

`A_t` \= current architecture state  
`d_t` \= disturbance or novelty pressure  
`u_t` \= stabilizing control response  
`F` \= system transition function

The stability question becomes:

Does the control response `u_t` keep the architecture inside the identity-preserving basin?

## **Governing Models**

This algorithm is derived from:

Phase 5B.1 — Identity Model

Phase 5B.2 — Stability Model

Core elements:

Identity Kernel:

`I_K(A) = {K, V, C, L, B}`

Where:

`K` \= constitutional invariants  
`V` \= verification continuity  
`C` \= coherence continuity  
`L` \= lineage traceability  
`B` \= boundary conditions of selfhood

Architectural Instability Function:

`V_A(A_t) = w_1E_C + w_2E_G + w_3E_V + w_4E_I + w_5E_M + w_6E_Q`

Where:

`E_C` \= coherence error  
`E_G` \= grounding error  
`E_V` \= verification drift  
`E_I` \= identity erosion  
`E_M` \= memory inconsistency  
`E_Q` \= governance conflict

The architecture is stable if:

`V_A(A_{t+1}) ≤ V_A(A_t)`

or, during temporary adaptive disruption:

`V_A(A_{t+n}) ≤ θ_S`

within a bounded recovery interval `n`.

## **Inputs**

The Stability Regulation Algorithm receives:

### **Current Architecture State**

`A_t`

The architecture before disturbance or transformation.

### **Disturbance Event**

`d_t`

The destabilizing input, pressure, contradiction, novelty, evidence update, memory conflict, or proposed change.

### **Projected Architecture State**

`A_{t+1}`

The expected state after disturbance and initial response.

### **Identity Kernel**

`I_K(A_t)`

The identity-preserving structures that stability must protect.

### **Current Stability Budget**

`SB_t`

The available capacity to absorb transformation without identity-threatening drift.

### **Architectural Instability Score**

`V_A(A_t)`

The current instability level before disturbance.

### **Disturbance Metadata**

Information about the disturbance, including:

source,

scope,

novelty level,

grounding status,

coherence impact,

memory impact,

identity risk,

verification impact,

governance impact,

and reversibility.

### **Thresholds**

Stability thresholds such as:

`θ_S` \= operational stability threshold  
`θ_C` \= constitutional risk threshold  
`θ_R` \= recovery interval threshold  
`θ_D` \= disturbance tolerance threshold  
`θ_SB` \= minimum stability budget threshold

## **Outputs**

The algorithm produces one of the following decisions:

### **Absorb**

The disturbance is within available stability budget and may be handled through ordinary correction.

### **Absorb with Monitoring**

The disturbance appears recoverable but requires post-response stability tracking.

### **Sandbox**

The disturbance or novelty is potentially useful but too destabilizing for direct integration.

### **Repair**

The architecture must activate coherence, grounding, memory, or verification repair before proceeding.

### **Delay**

The system should pause integration until stability budget recovers.

### **Rollback**

The disturbance or transformation has pushed the architecture beyond acceptable stability bounds.

### **Escalate**

The disturbance threatens identity, verification independence, constitutional invariants, or governance integrity.

## **Disturbance Classes**

The algorithm first classifies disturbance type.

### **External Disturbance**

New evidence, environmental change, adversarial input, tool output, source conflict, or reality correction.

### **Internal Disturbance**

Reasoning error, memory conflict, contradiction, symbolic drift, compression damage, or hallucinated relation.

### **Novelty Disturbance**

New hypothesis, speculative model, architectural alternative, analogy, counterfactual, or creative recombination.

### **Grounding Disturbance**

Evidence that weakens, contradicts, or revises existing claims.

### **Coherence Disturbance**

Conflict among symbolic structures, principles, memories, or active claims.

### **Persistence Disturbance**

Memory update, retraction, revision, demotion, or consolidation conflict.

### **Architectural Disturbance**

Change to reasoning procedures, verification mechanisms, grounding thresholds, memory rules, or scale couplings.

### **Constitutional Disturbance**

Pressure on invariants, governance authority, verification independence, or Identity Kernel components.

The deeper the disturbance reaches, the stronger the stability review.

## **Procedure**

### **Step 1 — Capture Pre-Disturbance Stability State**

Record:

`A_t`

`I_K(A_t)`

`V_A(A_t)`

`SB_t`

and current governance mode.

This baseline is necessary for determining whether disturbance produces recoverable motion or destabilizing drift.

Without baseline capture, the architecture cannot distinguish adaptation from erosion.

### **Step 2 — Classify the Disturbance**

Classify `d_t` according to type, depth, and scope.

Ask:

Is the disturbance external or internal?

Does it affect local reasoning, memory, architecture, or constitution?

Does it introduce novelty, contradiction, evidence, or self-modification pressure?

Does it affect the Identity Kernel?

Does it modify evaluators or only evaluated content?

Disturbance classification determines the required response pathway.

### **Step 3 — Estimate Disturbance Magnitude**

Estimate the destabilization load:

`DL(d_t)`

Disturbance load may include:

coherence disruption,

grounding disruption,

identity risk,

memory inconsistency,

verification drift,

governance conflict,

novelty pressure,

and reversibility risk.

A preliminary disturbance load function:

`DL(d_t) = αC_d + βG_d + γI_d + δM_d + εV_d + ζQ_d + ηN_d`

Where:

`C_d` \= coherence disturbance  
`G_d` \= grounding disturbance  
`I_d` \= identity disturbance  
`M_d` \= memory disturbance  
`V_d` \= verification disturbance  
`Q_d` \= governance disturbance  
`N_d` \= novelty disturbance

The disturbance is manageable only if disturbance load does not exceed available stability capacity.

### **Step 4 — Compare Disturbance Load to Stability Budget**

Evaluate:

`DL(d_t) ≤ SB_t`

If true, the disturbance may be absorbed or processed.

If false, the disturbance exceeds current stability budget.

The algorithm must then choose among:

sandbox,

delay,

repair,

rollback,

or escalation.

The Stability Budget prevents the system from allowing too much novelty, contradiction, or self-modification before consolidation occurs.

### **Step 5 — Project Post-Disturbance Architecture**

Generate projected state:

`A_{t+1}`

Estimate how the disturbance affects:

coherence relations,

grounding obligations,

verification continuity,

identity continuity,

memory structure,

governance integrity,

and constitutional invariants.

This projection does not need perfect prediction.

It must identify plausible destabilization pathways.

### **Step 6 — Compute Post-Disturbance Instability**

Compute:

`V_A(A_{t+1})`

Then compare:

`V_A(A_{t+1})` to `V_A(A_t)`

and to threshold:

`θ_S`

If:

`V_A(A_{t+1}) ≤ θ_S`

then the architecture remains operationally stable.

If:

`V_A(A_{t+1}) > θ_S`

then the architecture enters caution mode.

If:

`V_A(A_{t+1}) > θ_C`

then the architecture enters constitutional risk mode.

### **Step 7 — Check Identity Basin Boundary**

Determine whether projected state remains inside the identity-preserving basin:

`A_{t+1} ∈ B_I`

Where:

`B_I` \= identity-preserving basin.

The algorithm checks whether:

constitutional invariants remain intact,

verification continuity remains intact,

coherence recovery remains possible,

lineage remains traceable,

and boundary conditions remain protected.

If `A_{t+1}` exits `B_I`, the disturbance cannot be treated as ordinary instability.

It becomes identity risk.

Decision:

`Rollback` or `Escalate`

### **Step 8 — Determine Recovery Path**

If instability rises but remains recoverable, determine whether a bounded recovery path exists.

A bounded recovery path means:

`V_A(A_{t+n}) ≤ θ_S`

for some acceptable interval `n ≤ θ_R`.

Recovery operations may include:

coherence repair,

grounding review,

memory revision,

confidence adjustment,

hypothesis demotion,

novelty sandboxing,

rollback,

verification review,

or governance arbitration.

If no recovery path exists, the disturbance is not stable.

It is uncontrolled drift.

### **Step 9 — Select Stabilizing Response**

Select stabilizing control response:

`u_t`

Possible responses include:

absorb,

monitor,

sandbox,

repair,

delay,

rollback,

or escalate.

The selected response should reduce architectural instability while preserving adaptive capacity.

The architecture should not overcorrect.

Excessive correction may suppress useful novelty or valid grounding pressure.

The goal is not zero instability.

The goal is recoverable instability.

### **Step 10 — Apply Response or Route to Governance**

If the disturbance is low-risk and within stability budget, proceed with ordinary correction.

If the disturbance is moderate-risk, proceed with monitoring.

If the disturbance is useful but destabilizing, route to sandbox.

If the disturbance damages coherence, grounding, memory, or verification, route to repair.

If the disturbance threatens identity or constitution, route to governance escalation.

### **Step 11 — Update Stability Budget**

After response selection, update:

`SB_{t+1}`

Stability budget decreases when the architecture absorbs disturbance, novelty, contradiction, or self-modification.

Stability budget increases after successful grounding, coherence repair, verification, consolidation, and identity-preserving integration.

A simplified update form:

`SB_{t+1} = SB_t - Cost(d_t) + Recovery(u_t)`

Where:

`Cost(d_t)` \= destabilization cost of disturbance  
`Recovery(u_t)` \= stabilizing effect of response

The architecture should not permit continuous high novelty or modification without budget recovery.

### **Step 12 — Generate Stability Audit Record**

Create audit record containing:

baseline stability state,

disturbance classification,

disturbance load,

pre-disturbance instability score,

post-disturbance projected instability score,

stability budget before and after,

identity basin check,

recovery path,

selected response,

escalation decision,

monitoring requirement,

and rollback availability.

The audit record supports future stability learning.

## **Thresholds**

The Stability Regulation Algorithm uses several thresholds.

### **Operational Stability Threshold**

`θ_S`

Maximum acceptable instability during ordinary operation.

### **Constitutional Risk Threshold**

`θ_C`

Instability level at which ordinary adaptation must pause and constitutional review may be required.

### **Recovery Interval Threshold**

`θ_R`

Maximum acceptable time or number of cycles before instability must return below operational threshold.

### **Disturbance Load Threshold**

`θ_D`

Maximum tolerable disturbance load before direct integration is blocked.

### **Stability Budget Threshold**

`θ_SB`

Minimum stability budget required before novelty, persistence, or architectural modification can proceed.

## **Governance Modes**

The Stability Regulation Algorithm may trigger governance mode changes.

### **Normal Mode**

`V_A(A_t) ≤ θ_S`

Ordinary adaptation permitted.

### **Caution Mode**

`V_A(A_t) > θ_S`

Higher monitoring, reduced novelty budget, stronger verification.

### **Constitutional Risk Mode**

`V_A(A_t) > θ_C`

Ordinary adaptation pauses. Governance escalation required.

### **Recovery Mode**

Instability is elevated but a bounded recovery path exists.

### **Lockdown Mode**

Identity basin boundary is breached or verification integrity is threatened.

Only rollback, audit, repair, or constitutional governance may proceed.

## **Failure Modes**

### **Underreaction**

The algorithm treats identity-threatening drift as ordinary disturbance.

### **Overreaction**

The algorithm suppresses legitimate novelty or grounding pressure in the name of stability.

### **False Stability**

The architecture appears stable because contradiction, novelty, or evidence is suppressed.

### **Drift Accumulation**

Small disturbances individually pass review but cumulatively weaken identity continuity.

### **Budget Exhaustion**

The architecture continues novelty or modification after stability budget is depleted.

### **Recovery Illusion**

The system assumes recoverability without a concrete recovery path.

### **Oscillation**

The architecture overcorrects repeatedly between competing states.

### **Rigidity**

The system minimizes instability so aggressively that adaptation becomes impossible.

### **Collapse**

The architecture exits the identity-preserving basin and cannot restore continuity.

### **Recursive Destabilization**

The disturbance modifies the mechanisms responsible for evaluating stability.

This is the most dangerous failure mode because instability may become invisible to the system itself.

## **Governance Hooks**

The Stability Regulation Algorithm must escalate when:

disturbance load exceeds stability budget,

instability exceeds constitutional risk threshold,

identity basin boundary is threatened,

verification mechanisms are affected,

recovery path is unavailable,

# **Phase 6.4 — Grounding Evaluation Algorithm**

*The Grounding Evaluation Algorithm does not ask whether a claim sounds coherent. It asks what reality-linked evidence the claim is accountable to.*

## **Algorithm Name**

Grounding Evaluation Algorithm

Abbreviation:

`GEA`

## **Primary Question**

What is the evidence status of this symbolic structure?

## **Purpose**

The Grounding Evaluation Algorithm assigns epistemic status to symbolic structures by linking internal representations to evidence, observation, measurement, reliable source structures, experimental confirmation, or externally checkable reality constraints.

The algorithm protects the architecture from mistaking internal coherence for truth.

A claim may be elegant.

A claim may be useful.

A claim may fit existing memory.

A claim may be widely repeated.

A claim may stabilize the system’s current model.

None of these are sufficient for grounding.

Grounding asks a stricter question:

What external constraint supports this internal symbol?

The purpose of the Grounding Evaluation Algorithm is to determine whether a symbolic structure is ungrounded, speculative, internally coherent, partially grounded, strongly grounded, contradicted, or rejected.

## **Core Principle**

Grounding is not consensus.

Grounding is not confidence.

Grounding is not coherence.

Grounding is the evidential coupling between internal representation and external constraint.

A symbolic structure becomes grounded when the architecture can connect it to evidence under verifiable conditions.

In formal terms:

A symbol is grounded to the degree that its internal representation is supported by external evidence under verifiable constraints.

The Grounding Evaluation Algorithm therefore does not decide truth by fluency, familiarity, agreement, or internal fit.

It assigns epistemic status according to evidence quality, evidence structure, contradiction burden, uncertainty, and traceability.

## **Governing Models**

This algorithm is derived from:

Phase 5B.4 — Grounding Model

Phase 5B.3 — Novelty Model

Phase 5B.6 — Coherence Model

Phase 5B.9 — Constitutional Governance Model

Primary formal objects include:

`E(c_i) = {e_1, e_2, ..., e_n}`

`G_E = (C, E, R)`

Where:

`S(c_i)` \= total support strength

`D(c_i)` \= directness of evidence  
`R(c_i)` \= reliability of sources or measurements  
`Q(c_i)` \= quality of inference connection  
`X(c_i)` \= contradiction strength  
`U(c_i)` \= unresolved uncertainty

A claim is sufficiently grounded if:

`GS(c_i) ≥ θ_G`

domain relevance,

and vulnerability to distortion.

Assign evidence quality:

`Q(e_j)`

Evidence directly measuring the claim carries more grounding force than evidence that only indirectly suggests it.

A reliable source is useful.

A reliable source with direct evidence is stronger.

A reliable source repeating another source may not add independence.

### **Step 6 — Evaluate Source Independence**

Estimate:

`I(c_i)`

Ask:

Do the evidence items come from independent origins?

Or do they trace back to the same source?

Does apparent agreement represent genuine convergence or source collapse?

Many dependent sources may provide less grounding than a few independent sources.

The algorithm must detect circular reinforcement.

### **Step 7 — Evaluate Support Strength**

Estimate:

`S(c_i)`

Ask:

How strongly does the evidence support the claim?

Does it directly confirm the claim?

Partially support it?

Merely correlate with it?

Support a weaker version?

Support a related but different claim?

Support strength must be linked to claim scope.

Evidence for a narrower claim should not be inflated into support for a broader claim.

### **Step 8 — Evaluate Contradiction Strength**

Estimate:

`X(c_i)`

Ask:

What evidence conflicts with the claim?

How reliable is the contradiction?

Does it refute the claim entirely?

Does it qualify the claim?

Does it restrict its scope?

Does it reveal uncertainty?

Does it indicate that the claim should be demoted, revised, or rejected?

Contradiction does not always destroy a claim.

Sometimes contradiction forces refinement.

But contradiction must be represented explicitly.

### **Step 9 — Evaluate Unresolved Uncertainty**

Estimate:

`U(c_i)`

Ask:

What remains unknown?

What assumptions are unresolved?

What evidence is missing?

What is the confidence interval?

What would change the claim’s status?

A grounded claim may still contain uncertainty.

Grounding does not require omniscience.

It requires disciplined representation of support and uncertainty.

### **Step 10 — Check for Circular Grounding**

Evaluate:

`Circularity(c_i)`

Ask:

Is the claim supported by evidence that depends on the claim itself?

Does memory cite prior memory without external support?

Do multiple sources trace back to one unsupported origin?

Does internal coherence masquerade as external evidence?

If circularity exceeds threshold:

`Circularity(c_i) > θ_R`

then grounding status must be reduced or rejected.

### **Step 11 — Perform Bayesian Update**

Update confidence using available evidence.

Let:

`P(c_i)` \= prior confidence

`P(c_i | E)` \= posterior confidence after evidence

Bayesian update:

`P(c_i | E) = P(E | c_i)P(c_i) / P(E)`

This step adjusts belief according to evidence.

However, Bayesian confidence must remain subordinate to evidence quality.

A numerical confidence estimate without evidence quality is false precision.

### **Step 12 — Compute Grounding Score**

Compute:

`GS(c_i) = αS(c_i) + βI(c_i) + γD(c_i) + δR(c_i) + εQ(c_i) - λX(c_i) - μU(c_i)`

Where:

`S(c_i)` \= support strength  
`I(c_i)` \= evidence independence  
`D(c_i)` \= directness  
`R(c_i)` \= reliability  
`Q(c_i)` \= inference quality  
`X(c_i)` \= contradiction strength  
`U(c_i)` \= unresolved uncertainty

The claim is sufficiently grounded for its current use if:

`GS(c_i) ≥ θ_G`

But different uses require different thresholds.

### **Step 13 — Assign Epistemic Status**

Assign status based on grounding score, contradiction, circularity, uncertainty, and claim type.

Possible assignments:

`Ungrounded`

if little or no evidence exists.

`Speculative`

if meaningful but insufficiently supported.

`Internally Coherent`

if it fits the symbolic architecture but lacks external evidence.

`Partially Grounded`

if evidence exists but remains incomplete or contested.

`Strongly Grounded`

if evidence is strong, direct, independent, and reliable.

`Contradicted`

if significant evidence conflicts with the claim.

`Rejected`

if contradiction, circularity, or grounding failure exceeds allowable thresholds.

### **Step 14 — Propagate Belief Updates**

If `c_i` changes status, update dependent structures.

Propagation may affect:

subclaims,

models,

memories,

hypotheses,

persistent knowledge,

architectural principles,

and active reasoning.

For example:

If a foundational claim is demoted, dependent claims may require review.

If new evidence strengthens a claim, related structures may become more viable.

If a contradiction appears, coherence review may be triggered.

Belief propagation prevents the architecture from isolating evidence updates.

Reality pressure should move through the symbolic network.

### **Step 15 — Route Claim for Next Review**

Based on status, route the claim.

Possible routing:

`Ungrounded → Discard or Sandbox`

`Speculative → Novelty Sandbox`

`Internally Coherent → Grounding Required`

`Partially Grounded → Continue Evaluation or Limited Use`

`Strongly Grounded → Coherence Review or Persistence Review`

`Contradicted → Revision or Coherence Repair`

`Rejected → Archive or Remove from Active Belief`

If the claim affects architecture or constitution, route to higher review regardless of grounding strength.

Strong evidence does not automatically authorize architectural change.

It authorizes the next review stage.

evidence set,

evidence graph,

support relations,

contradiction relations,

source independence analysis,

circularity analysis,

uncertainty estimate,

Bayesian update,

grounding score,

epistemic status,

dependent structures affected,

routing decision,

and future review requirements.

The grounding audit record allows the system to explain why a claim was believed, doubted, revised, or rejected.

### **Persistence Grounding Threshold**

### **Architectural Grounding Threshold**

These transitions preserve epistemic humility.

Grounding status can rise or fall as evidence changes.

Grounding thresholds rise with authority.

# **Phase 6.5 — Persistence and Consolidation Algorithm**

*The Persistence and Consolidation Algorithm does not ask whether information should be stored. It asks whether a symbolic structure has survived enough evaluation to responsibly shape future cognition.*

## **Algorithm Name**

Persistence and Consolidation Algorithm

Abbreviation:

`PCA`

## **Primary Question**

Has this structure survived enough evaluation to shape future cognition?

## **Purpose**

The Persistence and Consolidation Algorithm determines whether a symbolic structure should enter long-term memory, remain temporary, be archived, be revised, be demoted, or be rejected.

The algorithm prevents the architecture from treating memory as passive storage.

A coherent intelligence does not preserve everything it encounters.

It selectively preserves symbolic structures that have survived enough grounding, coherence, verification, stability, and identity review to remain useful across time.

The purpose of persistence is therefore not accumulation.

The purpose is governed survival.

A symbolic structure becomes persistent only when it earns the right to influence future cognition.

## **Core Principle**

Information becomes persistent knowledge only after passing through state transitions governed by grounding, coherence, verification, stability, and identity constraints.

Persistence is not the accumulation of information.

It is the selective survival of symbolic structures across time.

In formal terms:

Persistence is a transition process through which a symbolic structure moves from temporary cognition into long-term memory while retaining epistemic status, lineage, dependency structure, and revision eligibility.

The architecture may think with uncertainty.

But it must remember with discipline.

## **Governing Models**

This algorithm is derived from:

Phase 5B.1 — Identity Model

Phase 5B.2 — Stability Model

Phase 5B.4 — Grounding Model

Phase 5B.5 — Persistence Model

Phase 5B.6 — Coherence Model

Primary formal objects include:

Symbolic structure:

`s_i(t)`

Symbolic field:

`S_t = {s_1, s_2, ..., s_n}`

Metadata:

`Meta(s_i) = {status, grounding, coherence, confidence, source, lineage, dependencies, revision history}`

Memory graph:

`G_M = (S, R)`

Where:

`S` \= symbolic memory nodes  
`R` \= relationships among symbols

Persistence Score:

`PS(s_i) = αG(s_i) + βC(s_i) + γV(s_i) + δR(s_i) + εU(s_i) + ζL(s_i) - λX(s_i) - μD(s_i)`

Where:

`G(s_i)` \= grounding strength  
`C(s_i)` \= coherence compatibility  
`V(s_i)` \= verification status  
`R(s_i)` \= reconstruction stability  
`U(s_i)` \= usefulness across reasoning contexts  
`L(s_i)` \= lineage clarity  
`X(s_i)` \= contradiction burden  
`D(s_i)` \= destabilization risk

A symbolic structure may enter persistent memory if:

`PS(s_i) ≥ θ_P`

and lineage, revision, grounding, and reconstruction conditions are satisfied.

## **Inputs**

The Persistence and Consolidation Algorithm receives:

### **Symbolic Structure**

`s_i`

The claim, hypothesis, memory candidate, model, principle, relation, compression, or reconstruction anchor under review.

### **Current Epistemic Status**

The current status assigned by grounding, novelty, coherence, or verification processes.

Grounding score and evidence status from the Grounding Evaluation Algorithm.

### **Coherence Evaluation**

Coherence score, unresolved tension status, contradiction burden, and graph compatibility.

### **Verification Status**

Whether the symbolic structure has passed appropriate verification for its type and intended authority.

### **Lineage Record**

The origin, reasoning path, evidence, revisions, dependencies, and prior status changes of the structure.

### **Memory Graph Context**

`G_M`

The existing persistent memory graph into which the structure may be added, linked, revised, archived, or rejected.

### **Stability State**

Current stability budget and destabilization risk.

### **Identity Constraints**

Identity Kernel relevance, especially if the structure may affect memory lineage, architectural principles, or future self-evaluation.

### **Thresholds**

Persistence thresholds such as:

## **Outputs**

The algorithm produces one or more of the following decisions:

### **Remain Temporary**

The structure remains in active cognition or working memory.

### **Sandbox**

The structure is promising but not ready for memory consolidation.

### **Archive**

The structure is stored as historical, speculative, deprecated, or contextual material without active knowledge authority.

### **Persist**

The structure enters persistent knowledge with epistemic status and lineage preserved.

### **Persist with Qualification**

The structure enters memory with scope limitations, uncertainty markers, or unresolved tensions explicitly attached.

### **Revise Before Persistence**

The structure requires refinement, qualification, grounding, or coherence repair before consolidation.

### **Demote**

A previously persistent structure loses active knowledge authority.

### **Retract**

A persistent structure is removed from active belief due to contradiction, grounding failure, or coherence failure.

### **Reject**

The structure fails review and should not influence future cognition except as a rejected or archived artifact.

### **Escalate**

The structure seeks architectural, invariant, or constitutional authority and must undergo higher review.

## **Persistence States**

A symbolic structure may occupy one of several persistence states.

### **Active Cognition**

The structure is being considered but has not entered memory.

### **Working Memory**

The structure is temporarily useful for the current task or reasoning context.

### **Sandbox Memory**

The structure is preserved for further exploration but carries no belief authority.

### **Speculative Archive**

The structure is saved as a possible future reference, clearly marked as speculative.

### **Historical Archive**

The structure is saved because it records developmental history, even if not currently believed.

### **Persistent Knowledge**

The structure is durable, usable, and authorized to influence future reasoning.

### **Qualified Persistent Knowledge**

The structure persists but carries scope, uncertainty, contradiction, or domain limits.

### **Deprecated Knowledge**

The structure previously had authority but has been superseded or weakened.

### **Rejected Artifact**

The structure is retained only as a record of error, contradiction, or failed reasoning.

### **Architectural Candidate**

The structure has persisted strongly enough to be considered for architectural elevation.

# **Phase 6.6 — Coherence Repair Algorithm**

The algorithm does not attempt to force all symbolic structures into agreement.

A coherent intelligence must be able to preserve unresolved problems, speculative possibilities, competing hypotheses, and productive tensions without collapsing into contradiction or fragmentation.

The purpose is to determine whether tension is:

## **Core Principle**

Consistency means the absence of direct contradiction.

Coherence means structured compatibility across relationships, scales, dependencies, and purposes.

A symbolic system may contain unresolved tension and remain coherent if that tension is recognized, bounded, localized, labeled, and subject to future repair.

A symbolic system may also contain no obvious contradiction and still be globally incoherent if its parts do not meaningfully integrate.

In formal terms:

Coherence is the degree to which symbolic structures remain mutually compatible within a constrained dependency network across multiple scales of reasoning.

# **Phase 6.8 — Architectural Evolution Algorithm**

*The Architectural Evolution Algorithm does not ask whether a change improves cognition now. It asks whether the change deserves to alter the machinery through which all future cognition will occur.*

## **Algorithm Name**

Architectural Evolution Algorithm

Abbreviation:

`AEA`

## **Primary Question**

Should this change modify the machinery through which future cognition occurs?

## **Purpose**

The Architectural Evolution Algorithm determines whether persistent knowledge, recurring failure patterns, stabilized principles, or proposed mechanisms may alter the architecture itself.

This algorithm does not govern ordinary learning.

Ordinary learning changes what the architecture knows.

Architectural evolution changes how the architecture comes to know.

A symbolic structure may become persistent memory without changing the architecture.

A persistent structure may become powerful enough to guide future reasoning.

A recurring failure may reveal the need for a new stabilization mechanism.

A repeated coherence repair may indicate a missing architectural function.

A persistent grounding problem may require changes to evidence handling.

When this happens, the architecture is no longer merely learning.

It is maturing.

The purpose of the Architectural Evolution Algorithm is to determine when such maturation is legitimate.

## **Core Principle**

An architecture evolves when persistent symbolic structures become governing structures.

Most cognition remains horizontal.

It moves through states such as:

`Observation → Candidate → Hypothesis → Grounded Claim → Persistent Knowledge`

Architectural evolution occurs when sufficiently stable knowledge moves vertically:

`Persistent Knowledge → Architectural Principle → Invariant → Constitution`

This is not ordinary memory consolidation.

It is structural promotion.

The architecture has not merely learned something.

It has changed what future learning must pass through.

In formal terms:

Architectural evolution is governed transformation of the architecture’s own transition functions, evaluation functions, memory structures, scale couplings, and constitutional constraints while preserving identity continuity.

## **Governing Models**

This algorithm is derived from:

Phase 5B.1 — Identity Model

Phase 5B.2 — Stability Model

Phase 5B.7 — Multi-Scale Reasoning Model

Phase 5B.8 — Architectural Evolution Model

Phase 5B.9 — Constitutional Governance Model

Primary formal objects include:

Architecture state:

`A_t = {S_t, M_t, R_t, G_t, V_t, C_t, K_t, Ω_t}`

`A_{t+1} = F_t(A_t)`

`F_t → F_{t+1}`

Architectural evolution operator:

`F_{t+1} = Φ(F_t, A_t, E_t, H_t, Ω_t)`

Architectural Fitness:

`AF(m_i) = αU(m_i) + βCoh(m_i) + γG(m_i) + δV(m_i) + εN(m_i) + ζR(m_i) - λI(m_i) - μS(m_i) - νK(m_i)`

`I(m_i)` \= identity risk  
`S(m_i)` \= stability cost

A modification is eligible for architectural review if:

# **Phase 6.9 — Constitutional Governance Algorithm**

*The Constitutional Governance Algorithm does not ask whether a decision is useful. It asks whether the decision has legitimate authority to alter, authorize, preserve, or constrain the architecture.*

## **Algorithm Name**

Constitutional Governance Algorithm

Abbreviation:

`CGA`

## **Primary Question**

Is this decision legitimate under the architecture’s constitution?

## **Purpose**

The Constitutional Governance Algorithm arbitrates among architectural domains, validates authority pathways, handles vetoes, escalations, amendments, constitutional risk, and legitimacy of deep architectural decisions.

This algorithm governs decisions that cannot be safely resolved by any single domain.

Novelty may propose.

Grounding may constrain.

Coherence may repair.

Persistence may consolidate.

Stability may regulate.

Identity may protect continuity.

Architectural evolution may propose structural change.

But no single engine may become sovereign over the whole architecture.

The purpose of constitutional governance is to ensure that deep decisions are not merely useful, powerful, efficient, coherent, or adaptive.

They must be legitimate.

A mature architecture must distinguish capability from authority.

## **Core Principle**

A mature intelligent architecture cannot be governed by capability alone.

It must be governed by constitutional legitimacy.

A process does not gain authority because it is useful, fast, confident, novel, coherent, or powerful.

It gains authority only when its action preserves the constitutional conditions that make coherent intelligence possible.

In formal terms:

Constitutional governance is a constrained decision system that allocates authority among architectural engines while preserving identity, stability, grounding, verification, coherence, and constitutional invariants.
